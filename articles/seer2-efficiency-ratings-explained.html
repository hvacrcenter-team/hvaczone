SEER2 vs SEER: What the 2023 Rating Change Means for Your AC

Since January 1, 2023, every new residential central air conditioner and heat pump carries SEER2, EER2, and HSPF2 ratings. Here's what actually changed, what the federal minimums are by region, and why the ENERGY STAR label just disappeared from central ACs.

Walk into any equipment quote since 2023 and the numbers look familiar but slightly off: SEER became SEER2, EER became EER2, and HSPF became HSPF2. The switch came from a new federal test procedure that took effect January 1, 2023, and the U.S. Department of Energy is blunt that "they are not directly comparable" to the old metrics ([DOE](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/appliance_standards/pdfs/2023_CAC_Standards_FAQ_10-5-2022_Final.pdf)). Here's what actually changed and how to read it.

Why the "2" — a tougher test, not a different machine

The "2" doesn't mark new equipment. It marks a new test procedure, codified in 10 CFR Part 430, Appendix M1, which AHRI Standard 210/240-2023 uses to rate residential central air conditioners and heat pumps ([AHRI](https://www.ahrinet.org/system/files/2024-09/AHRI%20Standard%20210.240-2023%20(2020)%20ICS.pdf)). The biggest practical change: conventional ducted systems are now tested at a minimum external static pressure of 0.50 in. of water column (w.c.), reflecting real-world duct resistance, where the older test ran at lower pressure ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/ENERGY%20STAR%20Version%206.1%20Central%20Air%20Conditioner%20and%20Heat%20Pump%20Final%20Specification%20(Rev.%20January%20%202022).pdf)).

Because the equipment works harder against that resistance in the test cell, the same unit tends to post a lower number under SEER2 than it did under SEER. ENERGY STAR's specification-development materials pair 14.0 SEER with 13.4 SEER2 and 16.0 SEER with 15.2 SEER2 as approximate equivalents ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/asset/document/ENERGY%20STAR%20Version%206.0%20Central%20Air%20Conditioner%20and%20Heat%20Pump%20Final%20Data%20Package_0.xlsx)) — but again, DOE stresses the two are not directly comparable.

Don't compare SEER to SEER2 across years.A 14 SEER unit from 2022 and a 14 SEER2 unit from 2024 are not the same animal — the test changed. When comparing quotes, line up SEER2 against SEER2 only.

The federal minimums (and why your region matters)

Since January 1, 2023, the nationwide floor for a split-system AC is 13.4 SEER2 and for a split-system heat pump 14.3 SEER2 / 7.5 HSPF2 ([DOE](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/appliance_standards/pdfs/2023_CAC_Standards_FAQ_10-5-2022_Final.pdf)). But split-system and single-package ACs also carry regional requirements: the Southeast and Southwest get a higher 14.3 SEER2 minimum (13.8 for units rated at 45,000 Btu/h or more), and the Southwest adds EER2 peak-efficiency requirements. The "North" region is defined as states with 5,000 or more heating degree days ([DOE](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/appliance_standards/pdfs/2023_CAC_Standards_FAQ_10-5-2022_Final.pdf)).

The 2026 news: ENERGY STAR dropped central ACs

Here's the current wrinkle. Effective February 1, 2026, the EPA removed residential central air conditioners from the ENERGY STAR central AC / heat-pump specification; air-source heat pumps remain covered, including a dedicated cold-climate tier ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/2025-12/ENERGYSTARInformationtoAssistEEProgramPlanningDec2025.pdf)). Practically, a new central AC won't carry the ENERGY STAR mark anymore — but a heat pump will, at SEER2 ≥ 15.2 ([ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/2025-04/ENERGY%20STAR%20Version%206.2%20Heat%20Pump%20Specification%20Rev.%20March%202025.pdf)). That lines up with where residential HVAC is heading: one electrified unit that heats and cools.

Rated efficiency is a ceiling, not a guarantee.The SEER2 number is what a matched system achieved in a test cell. Duct leakage, refrigerant charge, and airflow decide what actually shows up on your bill — that's where most efficiency is lost or preserved in the field.

How to actually use these numbers

  • Read the yellow EnergyGuide label. It lists the SEER2 (and HSPF2 for heat pumps) with a range bar against similar models. Fastest single spec check on any quote.
  • Get the AHRI Reference Number. For a split system, the rated efficiency only holds for a certified matched indoor/outdoor combination. Ask for the AHRI Certificate of Product Ratings and verify it in the AHRI Directory of Certified Product Performance ([AHRI](https://www.ahrinet.org/certification/cee-directory/air-conditioning-and-heat-pump-efficiency-101)). The wrong coil can drop a system below its rated SEER2.
  • Installed ≠ rated. A 15.2 SEER2 system on leaky, undersized ducts can deliver like a 13. A proper load calculation (Manual J) and decent duct condition matter as much as the sticker number.

The bottom line

SEER2 is the new common currency for AC and heat-pump efficiency. Read it only against other SEER2 numbers, mind your region's minimum, verify the AHRI matched-system certificate, and remember that installed efficiency — not the test-cell rating — is what reaches your utility bill.
