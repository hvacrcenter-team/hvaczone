HVAC Week in Review: July 13–19, 2026

It was a policy-and-supply-chain week. A federal housing law, a state refrigerant task force, a regional efficiency-standards bill, a grid-cost proposal, and a semiconductor investment all moved — and each one quietly reaches into how we heat, cool, and ventilate buildings.

The HVAC headlines this week weren't about equipment. They were about the rules around it, the buildings it goes into, the grid it plugs into, and the chips its controls run on. Here's what moved July 13–19, 2026, and why it matters for heating, cooling, and IAQ.

Policy & regulation

SMART Energy Efficiency Standards Act (S. 4892). A coalition of HVACR trade groups — ACCA, AHRI, HARDI, and PHCC — publicly backed Sen. Joni Ernst's bill, which would change the compliance date for regional HVAC energy-efficiency standards (furnaces, central air conditioners, heat pumps) from the date of installation to the date of manufacture ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55391172/hvac-trade-groups-back-smart-energy-efficiency-standards-act)). For contractors and homeowners, the practical effect — if it passes — is who bears the compliance burden when a unit is made in one region's tier and installed in another. (We unpacked how SEER2 regional standards work in our efficiency-ratings article.)

Washington ultra-low-GWP refrigerant task force. Washington's Department of Ecology stood up a Refrigerant Task Force under House Bill 1462 to study whether — and how — the state should move to ultra-low-global-warming-potential refrigerants by 2035, with HARDI holding a voting industry seat; the group meets monthly through 2027 and reports to the legislature for its 2028 session ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55391226/washington-launches-refrigerant-task-force-to-study-ultra-low-gwp-transition)). It's a study group, not a mandate — but it's the next chapter of the refrigerant-transition story we covered in our A2L / R-410A phase-down article, and worth tracking if you specify or service equipment in the Northwest.

Demand & housing

21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. Contracting Business reported that the ROAD to Housing Act became law on July 11 after the President declined to sign or veto it within the constitutional 10-day window, framing it as the first major federal housing legislation in roughly four decades ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55391390/how-the-new-road-to-housing-act-could-boost-hvac-installation-retrofit-and-service-demand)). The HVAC-relevant provisions: incentives for local governments to cut zoning and land-use barriers (more housing starts means more equipment), a pilot for aging-housing repairs that could pull retrofit and IAQ work, and a modernized definition of manufactured housing (removing the permanent-chassis requirement) that favors factory-installed HVAC in modular builds. More roofs, more units — eventually.

Grid & supply chain

Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340). A House subcommittee passed a bill that would require very large electricity customers — those with demand of 100 megawatts or more, the data-center class — to bear the full incremental cost of grid upgrades needed to serve them, including if they later walk away ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55391360/house-bill-would-shift-grid-upgrade-costs-to-large-electricity-users)). The HVAC angle is indirect but real: who pays for grid expansion shapes how fast electrification (heat pumps, EV charging) scales, and how rate burdens fall on residential vs. industrial loads.

$100B semiconductor investment. NIST announced an additional $100 billion in U.S. semiconductor investment ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/07/trump-administration-secures-additional-100-billion-us-semiconductor)). Modern HVAC depends on chips — board-level controls, inverter drives, sensors, and connected thermostats all run on them — so domestic capacity matters for lead times and parts availability, not for sticker prices directly.

The bottom line

The forces shaping HVAC this week came from outside the industry: housing policy, refrigerant climate goals, efficiency-compliance mechanics, grid cost-allocation, and chip supply. None changes what a good install looks like today — a right-sized load calculation, a compatible refrigerant, a clean drain, a filter the blower can handle — but several will change which equipment is available, how it's regulated, and how fast electrification scales over the next few years.
