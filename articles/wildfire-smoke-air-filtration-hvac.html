Wildfire Smoke & HVAC: Does Your System Actually Filter It?

Smoke is mostly fine particles (PM2.5), and not every air conditioner is built to catch them. Here's what MERV 13, portable HEPA, and DIY filters really do — and the gap between "the AC is on" and "the air is clean."

Wildfire smoke travels far from the fire. The EPA warned this month that outdoor smoke "can enter your home and make the air indoors unhealthy to breathe," and urged precautions once the Air Quality Index reaches unhealthy, very unhealthy, or hazardous levels ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-statement-cross-border-wildfire-smoke-and-air-quality-impact-us)). The pollutant to worry about is PM2.5 — fine particulate matter under 2.5 micrometers that slips deep into the lungs. A 2023 editorial in the American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine noted that wildfire smoke can account for as much as one-fourth of ambient PM2.5 in the United States ([Wu, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10806428/)).

Here's the catch most people miss: an air conditioner cools air, but it doesn't necessarily clean it. Whether your system filters smoke depends on the filter it holds and whether air is actually running through it.

Your HVAC + a MERV 13 filter

For central forced-air systems, the EPA recommends installing "the highest-efficiency filters your system can use to filter the PM2.5 in smoke," and specifically calls MERV 13 or higher filters best, if they are compatible with your HVAC system ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-06/heat-and-smoke-factsheet_1.pdf)). MERV (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value) is the ASHRAE 52.2 rating scale; a MERV 13 captures a meaningful share of fine particles that lower-rated fiberglass filters let through.

Two operating steps matter as much as the filter. During a smoke event, set the fan to "On" rather than "Auto" so air is cleaned continuously instead of only while cooling, and — if your system has the control — close the fresh-air intake or switch the system to "recirculate" so it isn't pulling smoky outside air in ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-06/heat-and-smoke-factsheet_1.pdf)). Both are temporary, smoke-event settings — not a reason to disable normal ventilation permanently.

MERV 13 only helps if air actually moves through it — and if your blower can push it.A higher-MERV filter raises static pressure (pressure drop), especially if it's undersized or loaded with dust. That can slow airflow, stress the blower, or even freeze the coil. Confirm your system can take the filter's pressure drop before you upgrade; we cover how static pressure is checked in our commissioning article.

Window units, portable ACs, and mini-splits usually don't filter smoke

This is the part that surprises people. The EPA notes that window air conditioners, portable air conditioners, and mini-split heat exchangers "typically do not have filters designed for the PM2.5 in smoke" — so running them won't clean the air the way a MERV 13 central system can ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-06/heat-and-smoke-factsheet_1.pdf)). For those setups — or for any room where you want cleaner air — the answer is a portable HEPA air cleaner.

Portable HEPA: the evidence

Peer-reviewed field work backs this up. A University of Washington study of Seattle homes during a 2020 wildfire episode found that auto-mode portable HEPA cleaners cut indoor PM2.5 by 48–78% after adjusting for outdoor levels and indoor sources — and that the PM2.5 infiltration factor ranged from 0.33 to 0.76, meaning "staying indoors ... is insufficient to prevent people's excess exposure to wildfire smoke" ([Xiang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8026580/)). A 2023 editorial put the typical reduction from HEPA purifiers at roughly 50–80% ([Wu, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10806428/)). Size the unit to the room using its Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR), and run it in the room you sleep in.

On a budget, the EPA also references a DIY portable air cleaner — a box fan taped to a high-efficiency furnace filter — as a low-cost fallback ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-06/heat-and-smoke-factsheet_1.pdf)). It's a reasonable stopgap, not a substitute for a properly sized HEPA unit, and it should be built and placed with fire safety in mind.

Filters catch particles, not all the smell.PM2.5 filtration targets the fine particles in smoke. It does not remove every smoke gas or odor. For gases and odors you need a sorbent stage such as activated carbon — a separate media, sized and maintained for the load.

What to actually do

  • Pick one room and make it the "clean room" — run a properly sized portable HEPA there during smoke events, with windows and doors closed.
  • If you have central air, upgrade to the highest MERV rating your system can handle (often MERV 13), run the fan "On," and recirculate if you can.
  • Change the filter when it's loaded — a smoke event can clog a filter in days. A loaded filter both stops cleaning and chokes airflow.
  • Verify with a monitor. A PM2.5 sensor tells you whether your setup is actually working. (We break down which numbers matter in our IAQ monitors article.)

The bottom line

"The AC is running" is not the same as "the air is clean." Wildfire smoke is a PM2.5 problem, and it takes a MERV 13 filter your blower can handle (run with the fan on) and/or a properly sized portable HEPA in the room you sleep in to make a real dent. Staying indoors alone isn't enough — the science is clear on that.
